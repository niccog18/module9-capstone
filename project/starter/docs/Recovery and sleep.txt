Recovery is the process by which the body repairs and adapts after training. Muscles get stronger during recovery, not during the workout itself, so adequate recovery is part of the training program.

Sleep is one of the most important recovery tools. A joint consensus statement of the American Academy of Sleep Medicine and the Sleep Research Society recommends that healthy adults sleep seven or more hours per night on a regular basis to support good health.

Rest days allow tissues to recover between hard sessions. Many programs schedule at least one or two rest days per week, and the right number depends on training volume, intensity, experience, and how well the lifter is recovering. Beginners often recover faster from lighter volumes, while very demanding programs may need more recovery.

Signs that recovery may be inadequate include performance that stays lower than usual for several sessions, persistent soreness, unusual fatigue, poor sleep, and low motivation to train. Reducing volume, adding a rest day, or taking a deload week are common responses.

Nutrition supports recovery. A meta-analysis of protein supplementation in resistance training found that daily protein intake up to about 1.6 grams per kilogram of body weight was associated with additional gains in muscle mass in healthy adults, with little added benefit beyond that on average. Adequate total energy and hydration also matter.

Sharp pain, pain that gets worse, or pain that lingers is different from normal muscle soreness and should be evaluated by a qualified health professional.
