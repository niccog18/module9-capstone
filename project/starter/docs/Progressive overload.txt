Progressive overload is the gradual increase of the demands placed on the body over time so that training continues to produce adaptation. If the same weights, sets, and repetitions are repeated indefinitely, the body adapts and progress slows. Progressive overload keeps the training stimulus challenging.

Progressive overload can be applied in several ways: increasing the weight lifted, performing more repetitions with the same weight, performing more sets, improving exercise technique or range of motion, shortening rest periods, or increasing training frequency. Adding weight is only one option, and beginners can often progress by adding repetitions first.

A common method is double progression. The lifter chooses a repetition range, for example 8 to 12 repetitions, and uses one weight until every set reaches the top of the range. Then the weight is increased slightly and the lifter works back up from the bottom of the range.

The American College of Sports Medicine position stand on resistance training progression suggests increasing the load by a small amount, about 2 to 10 percent, when a person can complete one to two repetitions beyond the current target on the last set in two consecutive sessions. Small, consistent increases are safer and more sustainable than large jumps.

Progress is not always linear. Sleep, stress, nutrition, and accumulated fatigue all affect performance. A session that does not beat the previous one is not a failure, and tracking workouts over weeks gives a clearer picture than judging any single day.

Reference: American College of Sports Medicine (2009). Progression models in resistance training for healthy adults. Medicine and Science in Sports and Exercise, 41(3), 687-708.
