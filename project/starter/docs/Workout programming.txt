Workout programming is the planning of exercises, sets, repetitions, load, and weekly structure. A training plan is organized into sessions, such as Push, Pull, and Legs, and each session contains exercises with a prescription for sets, repetition range, load, effort, and rest.

Common weekly structures include full-body sessions two to four times per week, upper and lower body splits, and push, pull, and legs splits. Full-body routines train every major muscle group each session and suit beginners or people with limited days. Splits divide the work across days so each session can include more exercises per muscle group.

Exercise selection usually starts with compound exercises that train several joints and muscle groups at once, such as squats, deadlifts, presses, rows, and pull-ups. Isolation exercises such as curls, triceps extensions, and calf raises are added after compound lifts to train specific muscles with less overall fatigue.

A balanced program includes movements that push, pull, and train the hips and knees, and it works all major muscle groups. Equipment, injury history, preferences, and time available all affect which exercises are practical, and a good substitute that can be performed consistently is better than a perfect exercise that is skipped.

A deload is a planned period, often about one week, in which training volume or intensity is reduced so accumulated fatigue can dissipate. Many programs include a deload every several weeks or whenever performance declines and fatigue builds up.

Planned training is different from completed training. A plan describes what a person intends to do, and a workout log records what was actually done, so comparing the two shows how well a plan matches real performance.
